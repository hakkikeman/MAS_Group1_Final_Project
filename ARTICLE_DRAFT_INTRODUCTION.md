# Introduction

Multi-agent systems (MAS) built on the Belief-Desire-Intention (BDI) model have long offered a principled framework for designing autonomous agents that reason, plan, and act in shared environments. Frameworks such as JaCaMo — which combines the Jason AgentSpeak interpreter, the CArtAgO artifact model, and the Moise organisational layer — provide mature infrastructure for deploying societies of interacting agents whose behaviour emerges from local rules and shared percepts. Yet BDI agents are, by design, bounded by the knowledge their developers encode. Their reasoning is transparent and auditable, but it is also brittle: an agent can only respond to situations its designers anticipated.

Large language models (LLMs) present a complementary profile. Trained on vast corpora of human-generated text, they exhibit flexible, context-sensitive reasoning across open-ended problems. They can articulate spatial strategies, weigh competing options, and explain their decisions in natural language — capabilities that BDI rule sets cannot easily replicate. The natural question is whether these two paradigms can be productively combined: can an LLM serve as the reasoning engine for a BDI agent, and if so, what does its decision-making look like under controlled, observable conditions?

This article addresses that question through a concrete simulation. We present a predator-prey multi-agent system modelled on a honeybee colony under wasp attack. The colony — implemented in JaCaMo with a queen, nurse bees, explorer bees, and sentinel defenders — operates according to conventional BDI rules. The predator, a single wasp agent, is different: its targeting decisions are delegated entirely to Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash model via a live API call at each decision cycle. The wasp receives a natural-language description of the current battlefield state — its own position, the positions of all sentinels, and the game rules governing attack radii and counter-attack thresholds — and must respond with a target coordinate and a one-line justification.

This setup allows us to observe LLM reasoning not in a benchmark or a chat interface, but inside a running multi-agent system where decisions have immediate, measurable consequences. We ask three specific questions. First, when does the LLM correctly identify danger from crowding — that is, when does it successfully avoid targets that would trigger a sentinel counter-attack? Second, when does it become overconfident, choosing targets it describes as "isolated" or "safe" that the simulation's ground truth classifies as dangerous? Third, does the LLM exhibit anything resembling a strategy that evolves across a battle — becoming more or less cautious as conditions change?

To answer these questions systematically, we conduct a parameter sweep across four configurations that vary the wasp's health, attack radius, movement speed, and the sentinels' counter-attack range and damage. Each run is logged at the decision level: every LLM call is recorded alongside the raw Gemini response, the chosen target, the number of sentinels within the attack and counter-attack zones, and a binary safety classification derived from the simulation's own rules. This structured logging enables quantitative analysis of LLM decision quality across varying environmental pressures.

Our results reveal a consistent pattern. The LLM reasons correctly about the *concept* of danger — its justifications consistently invoke spatial isolation, distance from the hive, and counter-attack risk — but its spatial estimates are unreliable. In overconfident decisions, the model declares a target safe while the simulation's sensor data shows two or more sentinels within counter-attack range. More interestingly, the LLM's decision quality changes within a single battle. In the configuration where the wasp ultimately wins, safe targeting improves from 36% in the first half of the battle to 100% in the second half — as the colony thins and isolated targets become easier to identify. In configurations where the wasp is overwhelmed from the start, the LLM's reasoning remains consistently wrong throughout, unable to adapt to parameters — such as the sentinels' counter-attack radius — that are not visible in its prompt.

These findings contribute to a growing body of work on LLM-augmented agent systems. They suggest that LLMs can add meaningful strategic reasoning to BDI agents in dynamic environments, but that their effectiveness is bounded by what they can perceive: an LLM that cannot observe its own health, the true density of enemies, or the parameters governing the environment it inhabits will reason fluently but incorrectly. Grounding — giving the LLM accurate, complete state information — emerges as the critical design variable.
